At a glance

The problem

Health information exists, but not in a form people can use

Millions of people in rural India and in low-income urban areas can't get reliable health guidance they understand. Most online health content is in English, full of medical jargon, and spread across apps they don't use. So people fall back on hearsay and forwarded WhatsApp messages, delay care, or take medicines incorrectly.

Programme brief: How can voice- and text-based AI agents give non-English-speaking and underserved communities better access to reliable health information?

Who we designed for

Asha, 62 · rural Uttar Pradesh
Basic phone, Hindi and a local dialect, weak internet. Finds long text hard to read and depends on others for health decisions.
Rajan, 34 · Mumbai, informal worker
Marathi and Hindi. A clinic visit costs him a day's wages, and he hesitates to ask sensitive questions in person.
Raju, 42 · farmer, Rajasthan
Hindi, 2G data, low literacy. Medical jargon confuses him and urban doctors are out of reach.
Lakshmi, 28 · migrant worker, mother
Tamil, and WhatsApp is her only internet. She needs quick checks for her children between shifts.

Key insight (Jobs-to-be-Done). People aren't looking for a diagnosis. They want clear next steps, in their own language, through a tool they already use every day.

The solution

Meet people where they already are: WhatsApp

  • No new app to install. WhatsApp is already on almost every phone in the target group.
  • Voice first. A large "speak" button and spoken replies for people with low literacy.
  • Guided, not open-ended. Tap-to-answer follow-up questions (symptom → duration → other symptoms) instead of a blank text box.
  • Always a human path. Home care, a clinic visit, or a one-tap connection to an ASHA worker or doctor.
  • A provider side. A dashboard where doctors and health workers review escalated cases.

My contribution · AI design

What the AI does, and what it must never do

I defined how Generative and Agentic AI would work together in the product. Each agent step mirrors a role in real healthcare, so the system stays predictable and safe.

1 · UnderstandSpeech-to-text in Indic languages, including mixed-language speech
2 · ClassifyDetect the intent: symptom, prevention, medicine or urgency
3 · Assess riskEmergency keywords, repeated questions or signs of distress trigger special handling
4 · Retrieve & simplifyGenAI turns verified medical content into plain language in the user's own language
5 · EscalateHand over to an ASHA worker or doctor whenever risk or uncertainty is high
The AI will
Translate and simplify verified health information · guide the next step · remind users about medicines · route people to human help
The AI will never
Diagnose · prescribe · replace a medical professional · keep more personal data than it needs

My contribution · Success metrics

How we'd know it's working

A health tool isn't social media: people only open it when they need it. So I set targets around trust and safe outcomes, not daily engagement.

Adoption & use
Activation (first completed guidance flow) · 30-day repeat use · DAU/MAU set at a realistic level for occasional use · share of sessions in local languages and by voice
Trust & safety
Accuracy in clinician spot checks (target above 90%) · correct escalation rate · a "was this helpful?" rating after each session · NPS
Outcomes
Share of users who follow the recommended next step within 2–3 days · response time for callbacks from an ASHA worker or doctor
Efficiency
Cost per conversation · cost of AI support compared with a human helpline

Process

How we got there

We moved from problem to MVP using standard product frameworks: Jobs-to-be-Done and the Value Proposition Canvas to frame user needs, SWOT, ERRC, Blue Ocean and SCAMPER to differentiate, a Lean Canvas for the business model, and MoSCoW and RICE to prioritise features. The design went from hand-drawn sketches through mid- and high-fidelity wireframes to two clickable prototypes. Context and market data came from public sources, including India's Ministry of Health & Family Welfare and the WHO.

MVP scope: multilingual voice and text, a guided symptom checker, and escalation. Out of scope: diagnosis, prescriptions, video consultations and medical-record integration.

Outcome & reflection

What happened, and what I'd do next

We submitted the PRD and presented it to the programme panel. The feedback singled out how we approached the problem and the WhatsApp-first idea as the strongest parts of the presentation.

If I took it further

  • Talk to real users first. Our research relied on public data. I'd run 5–8 interviews with people like our personas, reached through ASHA workers, before building anything more.
  • Replace the script with a real model, safely. Retrieval only from verified sources, with escalation tested against a set of red-flag scenarios before any launch.
  • Measure the right failure. The metric that matters most is missed escalations (a serious case the bot didn't route to a human), not accuracy on easy questions.
