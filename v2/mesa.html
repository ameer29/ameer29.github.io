Mesa: cook together, eat wider

Our challenge from SoReDI was youth loneliness in the age of AI. One sprint, twelve conversations, one live prototype. Our answer: a weekly table for students who just landed in a new city. You cook together on Saturday and share one table on Wednesday.

The live app

  1. Join with your university email. Mesa runs inside one university, so everyone at your table is someone you'll run into again.
  2. Get matched into a table of four passports. Four students from four countries, so nobody has the home advantage.
  3. Pick a dish. One from each of your countries, plus a few that anyone can manage.
  4. Saturday: cook together. A shopping list, jobs you can swap, and a recipe video.
  5. Wednesday: one table. Four kitchens bring four dishes. Halfway through comes "the round": each person names one thing they're grateful for and one thing that's hard.

This is the real app, embedded. It's interactive.

1 · The problem

One in four is lonely, and AI answers first

25.5%
of 16–29-year-olds in Spain feel lonely (ONCE, 2024)
6
in-depth interviews in week one, one per teammate
7
prototype tests: six students plus one AI walkthrough

Arriving in a new city is the worst moment to meet anyone. Welcome week throws hundreds of names at you at once, and by week three the groups have quietly closed. So people turn to AI instead. AI isn't a bad product; it's just a faster one. It's awake at midnight, it agrees with you, and the conversation ends feeling resolved.

What the interviews kept showing. Real comfort, no relationship, so the loneliness survived the conversation. One interviewee explained why she asked an AI, not a friend, to settle an argument: "Closure was faster." And nobody in six interviews used the word "lonely". They all said "busy".

2 · The user

She doesn't want 600 contacts. She wants two people.

Giulia, 23 · Milan · month 2 of 5 on exchange
A persona synthesised from our six interviews. Her programme friends feel temporary ("we're all leaving anyway"), so she holds back and the friendships stay polite. Evenings and the hour after an argument are the hardest, with nobody local to talk it through.
Her goal
One or two friendships that survive the semester, and the feeling of living in Barcelona rather than passing through it. Not a bigger network, just somewhere to turn up on a Wednesday.

My interview (Interview 6): a 25-year-old two to three weeks into Barcelona, an introvert who had learned to open up, who uses AI for work emails and translation and "sometimes like my psychologist" late at night, when there's nobody to call. The takeaway we built around: AI can be a companion, but not the only one.

3 · The solution

A weekly table, not another app to perform on

CookSix students from one university, verified by university email, cook a dish from home together. The task carries the conversation, so nobody has to be interesting on demand.
TableFour of those kitchens bring their dishes to one shared table, at a new venue every week. You eat all four, not just your own.
The roundEveryone names one thing they're grateful for and one thing that's hard. No fixing, and no advice unless someone asks for it. Everyone takes a turn, so nobody is exposed.

Paper sketch on Day 2, clickable and in front of students on Day 3.

4 · Iteration

Seven tests found the flaw that would have killed it

We ran the Explorative Experimentation Cycle (Hassi & Rekonen, 2018): six hypotheses (would people host, download, show up, cook with strangers, feel safe, stay in touch), a ten-question script, and 10–15 minute sessions.

What testers told usWhat we changed
Nobody trusts a free sign-up. Testers admitted they'd cancel for better plans.Put money on the table: ingredients paid upfront (€5–8). It isn't revenue; it's the difference between an intention and a commitment.
Hosting alone is the real ask. Cooking with strangers was never the blocker; opening your own flat to five of them was.Show the people first: verified profiles and the full guest list before dinner, plus a co-host or a neutral kitchen.
Safety is about people, not the app. Testers wanted to see who was coming and where.(Covered by the guest list above.)
A download is a barrier; a university link isn't.Arrive through the university: a web app, with nothing to install.
Dietary needs and awkward seating.Make the next table better: ratings after each dinner feed the seating, and a recipe-and-budget matcher handles diets.

What we deliberately kept. Cooking together and the round. Every tester liked the shared task, so we didn't let ourselves redesign it.

5 · Final

Mesa v2 runs end to end

Sign up with a university email → profile (photo, studies, diet) → book and pay €5–8 → see the host and guest list → cook, eat and do the round → rate and swap contacts. The thread stays open, and the table comes back seven days later.

Our ask: a pilot. Four tables at ESADE in week three of the semester, not week one, because testers told us the first week is noise and the need arrives once everyone else has settled in. One number decides it: how many people book a second table.

"AI can give Giulia closure in thirty seconds. We're asking for two hours, one table, and four people who will still be there next Wednesday." (our closing line)

My contribution

From the whiteboard to the live product

  • Research and ideation, as a team. I ran one of the six user interviews and worked through the design-thinking process with the team on our shared Miro board: context map, research plan, interviews, empathy map, problem statement, How-Might-We, ideation, paper prototype and experiment plan.
  • I owned the website. Once we split the work, I built the web app, from the first clickable prototype we tested on Day 3 to the live version. I deployed it on Vercel and connected a Supabase database so we could collect real responses from ESADE students.
What students can do
A 6-step onboarding (university email, home country, what brings you here, cooking level) · table matching · dish picks · a weekly plan with a shopping list and swappable roles · the round · a group thread that stays open between weeks
What we learn from it
Feedback after every evening ("Did you feel more connected?", "Would you turn up next Wednesday?") and on the app itself, plus a private admin dashboard that exports every sign-up, dish choice and response to Excel or CSV

In the prototype, the matching is simulated; the planned logic uses diet, budget and neighbourhood. Privacy by design: anything shared at the table is opt-in, and the admin data needs a private key that is never in the public code.
